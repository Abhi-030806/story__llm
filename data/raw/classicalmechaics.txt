Classical mechanics constitutes the foundational framework of macroscopic physics, governing the motion of bodies under the influence of forces. Its domain strictly encompasses physical systems where the velocities involved are significantly less than the speed of light, thereby excluding relativistic effects, and where the action is large compared to Planck's constant, rendering quantum mechanical effects negligible. At its core, classical mechanics seeks to provide a deterministic description of nature. Given the initial state of a closed physical system—defined by the positions and momenta of all its constituent particles—and a complete knowledge of the fundamental forces acting upon them, the future and past states of the system can be predicted with absolute certainty. This deterministic worldview is encapsulated within the differential equations of motion that form the bedrock of the discipline.

Kinematics, a distinct subfield of classical mechanics, is concerned exclusively with the geometric and mathematical description of motion, entirely divorced from the physical causes—namely, forces and masses—that generate such motion. It establishes the language necessary to quantify trajectories, providing definitions for position, displacement, velocity, and acceleration. In kinematics, a particle is idealized as a point mass, possessing a spatial location but no spatial extent. The state of a particle in three-dimensional space is defined by a time-dependent position vector, typically referenced from a chosen coordinate system's origin. The displacement vector represents the change in position over a specific time interval.

The time evolution of the position vector yields the velocity of the particle. The instantaneous velocity is formally defined as the first derivative of the position vector with respect to time. Velocity is inherently a vector quantity, possessing both magnitude, termed speed, and direction. When a particle undergoes uniform motion, its velocity vector remains constant in both magnitude and direction, implying a linear trajectory. However, most physical systems exhibit non-uniform motion, necessitating the definition of acceleration. Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, calculated as the first time derivative of the velocity vector, or equivalently, the second time derivative of the position vector. An object accelerates if its speed changes, its direction of motion changes, or both occur simultaneously.

The mathematical formulation of kinematics heavily depends on the choice of the coordinate system. While Cartesian coordinates are ubiquitous for rectilinear motion, many physical problems possess intrinsic symmetries that make other coordinate systems far more elegant. For example, uniform circular motion is most naturally described using two-dimensional polar coordinates. In this framework, the position vector is specified by a radial distance from the origin and an angular coordinate. The velocity vector in polar coordinates resolves into a radial component, representing the rate of expansion or contraction, and a transverse or tangential component, determined by the angular velocity. Even when moving at a constant speed in a circle, a particle experiences a continuous centripetal acceleration directed toward the center of the circular path, purely due to the continuous change in the direction of the velocity vector. In three dimensions, cylindrical and spherical coordinate systems introduce corresponding geometric complexities, utilizing unit vectors that are themselves position-dependent and time-varying, demanding rigorous application of the chain rule in differential kinematics.

The transition from kinematics to dynamics requires the introduction of inertial mass, a fundamental property of matter that quantifies an object's resistance to changes in its state of motion. Dynamics explores the causal relationships between applied forces and the resulting kinematic acceleration. This relationship is elegantly encapsulated in Newton's three laws of motion, which form the axiomatic basis of classical mechanics. Newton's First Law, the law of inertia, postulates the existence of inertial reference frames—frames of reference that are either at rest or moving at a constant velocity. In such frames, an object subject to zero net external force will maintain a constant velocity vector.

Newton's Second Law provides the fundamental equation of motion, stating that the net external force acting on a body is directly proportional to its acceleration, with the mass acting as the constant of proportionality. More rigorously, it states that the net force equals the time rate of change of the body's linear momentum, a formulation that remains valid even for systems where the mass varies with time. Newton's Third Law states that all forces occur in pairs; if object A exerts a force on object B, object B simultaneously exerts a force of equal magnitude and opposite direction on object A. This principle of action and reaction is inextricably linked to the conservation of linear momentum.

When extending classical mechanics from single particles to multiparticle systems, the concept of the center of mass becomes paramount. The center of mass is a unique, mass-weighted geometric point within a system. Remarkably, Newton's Second Law applies to the entire system as if all its mass were concentrated at this single point and all external forces were applied directly to it. The internal forces between constituent particles, according to the Third Law, cancel out entirely and do not affect the acceleration of the center of mass. By adopting a reference frame centered on the center of mass—often referred to as the zero-momentum frame—the analysis of complex interactions, such as classical scattering or two-body collisions, is immensely simplified, as the total linear momentum of the system within this specific frame is perpetually zero.

Rotational kinematics and dynamics mirror their translational counterparts but operate with angular variables. Angular displacement, angular velocity, and angular acceleration replace linear position, velocity, and acceleration. The dynamic equivalent of force is torque, a pseudo-vector defined as the cross product of the position vector and the applied force. The inertial resistance to rotational acceleration is quantified not by a simple scalar mass, but by the moment of inertia. For rigid extended bodies, the moment of inertia is mathematically formalized as a second-rank tensor, reflecting the fact that an object's rotational inertia depends heavily on the spatial distribution of its mass relative to the specific axis of rotation. The relationship between torque and angular momentum is analogous to the translational relationship between force and linear momentum.

The deepest insights of classical mechanics arise from its conservation laws. In any isolated system free from external, non-conservative forces like friction, certain physical quantities remain invariant over time. The conservation of linear momentum arises directly from the translational invariance of space. The conservation of angular momentum is a consequence of the rotational invariance, or isotropy, of space. Finally, the conservation of mechanical energy—the sum of the kinetic and potential energies—holds true for systems governed entirely by conservative forces, and is deeply linked to the time-translation invariance of the physical laws governing the system. These conservation principles transcend the Newtonian framework, remaining valid in both relativistic and quantum mechanical regimes, thereby serving as the ultimate unifying threads throughout the entire tapestry of physics.
